Why bitcoin is holding above $67,000 — and what could break it

Spot ETF flows have returned, leverage is contained and the order book is thinner than it looks. Three forces are keeping the bid alive.

By Lena Ortiz 20 November 2026 · 18:20 UTC 8 min read

Bitcoin has traded in a $3,000 range for most of November. Chart: ChainMarket.

For the third week in a row, bitcoin has closed above $67,000. That is not a dramatic number on its own. What matters is how it got there: spot volumes are down, perpetual funding is close to neutral, and the coins sitting on exchanges are the lowest they have been in six years.

The result is a market that looks calm on the surface and fragile underneath. Small bids move the price further than they used to, and the same is true of small offers.

The ETF bid is back

After two months of outflows, the spot ETF complex recorded four straight days of net creations last week. The total is modest — under $700 million — but the direction matters more than the size. Authorised participants tend to buy on the open and sell into strength, which softens rallies and cushions dips.

What is different this time is the mix. A larger share of the flows is coming from model portfolios rather than trading desks, according to data from three issuers. That money is slower to leave, and it does not use leverage.

“The marginal buyer this year is a portfolio allocator, not a trader. They do not panic on a 5% drawdown, but they also do not chase a breakout.”

— Mara Chen, head of digital assets at Northbridge Capital

That helps explain the range. Allocators buy when price dips toward their target weight and stop buying when it runs. The result is a market that mean-reverts instead of trending.

Leverage is not the story this time

In previous cycles, a quiet spot market usually hid a loud derivatives market. Not now. Open interest across the major venues is down 18% from its September peak, and funding has spent most of the month between 0.004% and 0.011% per eight hours — close to the cost of carry.

Liquidations have followed the same pattern. Only one day in the past thirty saw more than $300 million cleared, compared with six days in the same period last year. Traders are smaller, slower and less crowded.

That is healthy, but it also removes the fuel for a sharp squeeze. A move above $70,000 would need new spot demand, not a short liquidation.

The supply overhang is thinner than it looks

  • Exchange balances are at their lowest since 2018.
  • Long-term holders control roughly 74% of the circulating supply.
  • Miner selling has slowed since the halving, though margins remain tight.

The flip side is that the coins are not gone. They sit in custody, in ETFs and in corporate treasuries, all of which can become sellers if prices rise fast enough. The overhang has moved, not disappeared.

What could break it

Three things would change the picture. First, a sustained rise in real yields would make the carry trade less attractive and pull allocators back. Second, a regulatory surprise — positive or negative — would reprice the ETF premium quickly. Third, a large custody failure would test whether the new buyer base actually behaves differently from the old one.

None of these is imminent. But a market this thin does not need a large shock to move. It needs a small one at the wrong moment.

For now, the balance of evidence says the $65,000–$68,000 range holds. The interesting question is not whether bitcoin can reach $70,000. It is what happens to the order book when it tries.
